# Format: all-hands, town hall, internal update, change announcement

The audience is everyone: every function, level and tenure, with very different context. They want to know where the company is going, what it means for them, and whether leadership is being straight with them. Research specific to all-hands meetings is sparse; this guidance is practice informed by the general findings on mixed audiences, narrative and change communication.

## The questions in every head

1. What is happening, and why?
2. What does it mean for me and my team?
3. What do you want me to do?
4. Are you telling me the truth?

A good all-hands answers them in that order. Metrics dumps answer none.

## Spine for a regular all-hands

1. **Open with the headline:** the one thing to remember from this meeting.
2. **Where we are:** three or four numbers that matter, each with what it means and whether it is good. Not every dashboard. See `data-visuals.md`; big numbers work well.
3. **What we learned or what changed:** including what did not go well, stated plainly.
4. **Where we are going and why:** priorities for the next period, and what we are choosing not to do.
5. **What it means for you:** by team or role where it differs.
6. **Recognition:** specific people or teams, specific work, the impact it had. A customer story told through the people who did it is more memorable than a list of names.
7. **Q&A:** real time for it, including hard questions.
8. **Close:** the headline again and the one ask.

## Spine for a change or hard-news announcement

1. **The news, immediately and plainly.** Do not warm up; people sense it and stop listening until it arrives.
2. **Why:** the honest reasoning, including the alternatives considered.
3. **What changes and what does not,** and when.
4. **What it means for you:** as specifically as possible, including what is not yet known and when it will be.
5. **What happens next:** concrete steps, where to ask questions, who to talk to.
6. **Acknowledge the cost.** Do not spin or rush to the upside.

Slides for hard news should be minimal and plain: few words, no celebratory visuals, no clever animation. Offer the user a written follow-up, since people will not retain details heard under stress.

## Mixed-audience rules apply

Read `audiences.md`. In particular: no unexplained acronyms or team-internal names, translate each number into meaning, and say explicitly which parts concern which groups. Design for the newest person in the least-connected team; everyone else will still follow.

## Several presenters

All-hands decks are often assembled from sections by different people. Give each section the same shape (headline, evidence, what it means), one consistent visual language, a time budget, and a handover line in the notes. Agree the single overall headline first so sections add up to something.

## Tone

Direct, human and calibrated. Celebrate specifically, admit misses specifically. Corporate euphemism reads as evasion, and trust lost in an all-hands is expensive to rebuild.

## Remote and hybrid

Most all-hands are at least partly remote: plan interaction every few minutes (poll, chat prompt, live Q&A tool), keep slides legible on a laptop, and make sure remote questions get equal time. The deck will be shared afterwards, so assertion titles need to carry the message for people who skim it later.

## Pitfalls

- Every team gets a slide to show what they did.
- Numbers without interpretation.
- Burying bad news in the middle.
- Recognition as a list of names.
- Strategy slides that could belong to any company.
- No time left for questions.
