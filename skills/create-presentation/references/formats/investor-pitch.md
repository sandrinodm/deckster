# Format: investor pitch, fundraising, internal budget pitch

The audience is evaluating a bet under uncertainty: is the outcome big, is this the team, why now, and what is the evidence? They see many pitches and decide quickly whether to keep paying attention. For an internal budget or business-case pitch to executives, combine this with `executive-briefing.md`.

## What the evidence says, and how weak it is

- **Decks are skimmed [vendor data].** DocSend's analyses of deck viewing report average investor time on a deck of roughly two to four minutes depending on year and stage, with the most time going to sections such as business model, traction, team and competition, and figures shifting year to year. The exact numbers are from a vendor with a selected sample; the reliable message is that a sent deck gets minutes, so every slide must make its point from the title alone.
- **Section conventions are convention [practice].** Sequoia's widely used outline (purpose, problem, solution, why now, market, competition, product, business model, team, financials) is practitioner guidance, and investors themselves say clarity of thinking matters more than the template. Following the convention helps because investors know where to look; it is not evidence that the order persuades.
- **Rules like "10 slides, 20 minutes, 30-point font" are opinion.**
- General findings apply: explicit conclusions, two-sided handling of risks, and message titles on data.

## Two decks, not one

- **Sent deck (read-alone):** will be skimmed without you. Assertion titles that tell the story by themselves, slightly more explanatory text, about 10 to 15 slides.
- **Presented deck:** sparser, you carry the detail, built for interruption and a conversation.

Ask which one the user needs; offer both if they are raising.

## Spine

1. **Purpose:** what the company does in one plain sentence. No jargon, no "Uber for".
2. **Problem:** who has it, how painful, how they cope today. A real customer story if available.
3. **Solution and product:** what you built and why it is better, shown.
4. **Why now:** what changed (technology, regulation, behaviour) that opens the window.
5. **Traction:** the strongest evidence of pull you have: revenue, growth, retention, usage, pilots, waitlist. Real numbers, honestly charted.
6. **Market:** who buys, how many, how much, built bottom-up from real units and prices.
7. **Business model:** how you make money, key unit economics.
8. **Competition:** honest landscape including the status quo, and why you win.
9. **Team:** why these people for this problem.
10. **Financials and plan:** key projections with stated assumptions.
11. **The ask:** one amount, never a range, what it buys, and the dated milestones it reaches.

Order by strength. If traction is exceptional, lead with it after the purpose. If the team is the reason to believe, move it up. The first two minutes should contain your best evidence.

Several beats have a layout of their own: the product as a `screen`, a named customer's result as a `proof`, the market as an `equation`, the competition as a `matrix`, the team with an `edge` line for each founder, and the ask as an `ask` (`slide-craft.md`, section 3).

## Content rules

- **Every number is real and sourced.** Never invent or inflate traction, market size, growth rates, customer names or logos. Investors check, and one wrong number discredits the rest. Use `[NEEDS: ...]` for anything not supplied.
- **Traction charts tell the truth:** consistent time axis, bars from zero, label whether it is revenue, bookings, GMV or signups, cumulative charts labeled as cumulative. See `data-visuals.md`.
- **Market sizing bottom-up:** number of customers times price, with the source. A giant top-down number with "if we get 1%" signals weak thinking.
- **Name the risks** and say what you are doing about them. Investors will find them anyway; raising them shows judgement.
- **One message per slide, in the title:** "Net revenue retention is 128%" rather than "Traction".
- **A story they can retell.** The partner in the room must pitch you to partners who were not. Give them the one-sentence version and the three reasons.

## Internal budget pitch

Same logic, different vocabulary: the problem and its cost to the business, the proposal, expected return with assumptions, options including doing nothing, risks, the ask. Use answer-first ordering.

## Notes for this format

The spoken one-line version of each slide, the story behind each number, and "If asked" for the hard questions: churn, burn, competition, valuation, why you. An appendix with cohort data, pipeline, detailed financials, product detail and customer references.

## Pitfalls

- Starting with the solution before the problem is felt.
- Vanity metrics and cumulative charts presented as growth.
- A competition slide claiming no competitors.
- Jargon-heavy purpose statements.
- An ask with no amount or use of funds.
- Team slide as a wall of logos with no "why us".
