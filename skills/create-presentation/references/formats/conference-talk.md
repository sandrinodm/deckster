# Format: conference talk, keynote, meetup, lightning talk, webinar

The audience chose to attend, owes you nothing, and has a phone. They want to leave with one idea worth repeating and to feel the time was well spent. Evidence specific to conference talks is thin; this guidance applies the general research (narrative, multimedia principles, attention) plus established practice.

## One idea

A talk can carry one main idea and about three supporting points. Write the idea as a sentence someone would say to a colleague afterwards: "You should test your backups by restoring them, because ours were silently broken for a year." Everything in the talk either builds that idea or gets cut. The material you cut is what makes the rest good.

Pitch the talk to what the audience can use, not to what you did. "What we built" becomes "what you can take from what we built".

## Spine options

- **Story with lessons:** the situation, what went wrong or what was hard, the turn, what you learned, how the audience can apply it. Best for experience reports and postmortems. Specific and honest beats polished.
- **Problem, insight, how, proof, takeaway:** best for technique or tool talks.
- **Contrast (what is / what could be):** best for keynotes and vision talks.
- **Myth and reality:** three things people believe, why each is wrong, what to do instead.

Shape for a 30-minute slot: hook (1 to 2 min) → why this matters to you (2 to 3) → three sections of about 6 to 7 minutes, each with its own point, example and recap → synthesis and takeaway (2 to 3) → Q&A if scheduled. Do not plan content for the Q&A time.

## Openings and closings

- Start with the hook: the incident, the number, the question. Your name and employer are on the title slide; one line of credibility after the hook is enough.
- No long agenda. A single sentence of roadmap is plenty under 20 minutes.
- Close by restating the idea and giving one concrete thing to do on Monday. The last slide stays up: takeaway, a link or QR code to slides and resources, how to reach you.

## Slides for a big room

- Assume people at the back, a poor projector, and attention split with a laptop. Fewer words, bigger evidence, strong contrast.
- More slides, each simpler. A slide every 20 to 40 seconds in a story section is normal.
- Code: only the lines that matter, highlighted, readable from the back. Link to the rest.
- Live demos and live coding are high-risk on conference networks. Use a recording you narrate live unless the liveness is itself the point, and have the fallback regardless.
- Slides get shared and photographed. Assertion titles make a photographed slide meaningful on its own.
- Make the deck work without sound for the recording's thumbnail scrubbers: titles carry the story.

## Time formats

- **Lightning (5 minutes):** one idea, one example, one ask. About one sentence per slide. No intro, no agenda.
- **Short (15 to 20):** one idea, two or three points.
- **Standard (30 to 45):** as above; plan a change of mode (demo, story, question to the room) between sections.
- **Keynote (45 to 60):** broader audience, less detail, more story and contrast; a clear emotional peak.
- **Webinar:** remote rules apply. Interaction every few minutes (poll, chat question), simpler builds, and a deck that still works for people who only half-watch.

## Notes for this format

Fuller script-style notes are useful for rehearsing to time, especially the opening and the close, which are worth knowing nearly by heart. Mark the cut points for running over. Add the repeat-the-question reminder for Q&A.

## Pitfalls

- Company or product pitch disguised as a talk; audiences punish it.
- Chronological "what we did" with no lesson for the listener.
- Too much content; cutting during the talk instead of before it.
- Inside jokes and unexplained internal names.
- Ending on "Questions?".
