# Format: executive or board briefing, decision request, business review

The audience is senior, short on time, switching in from something unrelated, and will interrupt. They are there to make a decision or to be confident one is not needed. The measure of success is a clear decision, quickly.

## Answer first

Use the pyramid (see `narrative.md`): the recommendation and the ask in the first minute, then the reasons, then the evidence for each. Explicit recommendations persuade better than leaving the conclusion to the audience **[evidence]**, and executives who must wait for the point will go looking for it by interrupting.

The exception is a recommendation the room is primed to reject. Then open with the shared problem and the decision criteria, get agreement on those, and give the answer immediately after.

## Spine

1. **The ask and the answer:** what you recommend, what you need (decision, money, people, air cover), by when. One slide; when the need is an amount, the `ask` layout sets it with the dated milestones it buys.
2. **Why now / what is at stake:** the situation and complication in two or three sentences. One slide.
3. **Reasons:** two to four, each one slide with its evidence. Financial impact, customer or market evidence, risk.
4. **Options considered:** two or three real alternatives, including doing nothing, compared on the criteria that matter, with the recommendation marked. Executives decide between options; a single option invites them to invent their own.
5. **Risks and what would change your mind:** the top two or three, with mitigation. Raising them yourself is more persuasive than having them raised **[evidence, two-sided messages]**.
6. **Plan and cost:** milestones, owners, resources, how success will be measured.
7. **The ask again**, in the same words.
8. **Appendix:** the detail behind every number and the answers to likely questions.

For a **status or business review** with no decision: lead with the headline (on track, at risk, off track and why), then what changed since last time, then where you need help. Exceptions first, not a tour of every metric.

## Design for interruption

- **Each slide stands alone.** The discussion may jump to slide 6 and never return.
- **Core in few slides.** Plan to present for a third to half of the slot, five to eight core slides for a 30-minute meeting, and leave the rest for discussion.
- **The first slide must work as the whole briefing** if the meeting is cut to five minutes.
- **Deep appendix.** For every claim, a backup slide with the data. Title backups as assertions so you can find them under pressure. Mention that it exists. Backups sit after the closing slide with a token time budget (`framework.md`, Time budgets).
- **No section slides.** Five to eight core slides have no parts to divide; a divider spends a slide and half a minute on nothing. Use the eyebrow to label what each slide is (the ask, cost, risk).
- **Time.** Present for a third to a half of the slot and set the deck's `duration` to that share; the rest is their questions.
- **Send a pre-read** when the culture allows. A read-alone version needs more text on the slide (see "Delivery mode" in SKILL.md). Some organizations prefer narrative memos over slides for exactly this reason: prose exposes gaps in reasoning that bullets hide. If the user's organization works that way, offer a memo plus a short deck.

## Content rules

- **Numbers with context:** versus plan, versus last period, versus benchmark. A number alone means nothing to someone who does not live in the data. See `data-visuals.md`; big numbers and small tables are common here.
- **So what on every slide.** The title states the implication, not the topic.
- **Their vocabulary:** revenue, margin, risk, customers, time. Translate technical detail into consequence.
- **Calibrated confidence.** Say what is known, estimated and unknown. Give ranges for forecasts.
- **Trade-offs explicit.** What you are choosing not to do.
- **No surprises on bad news:** state it first, plainly, then cause, then plan.

## Notes for this format

Short. The opening sentence that states the ask, the one-line version of each slide, and "If asked" with the likely drill-down questions and which appendix slide answers each.

## Pitfalls

- Building up to the recommendation chronologically ("first we looked at…").
- Showing the work to prove effort.
- No explicit ask, or an ask without an amount or a date.
- Only one option.
- Hiding the risk.
- Dense "everything" slides with tiny type; put it in the appendix.
- Reading the slides to people who read faster than you talk.
