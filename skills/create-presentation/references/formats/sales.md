# Format: sales pitch and sales enablement

Two related jobs. Decide which one you are doing, or whether it is both.

- **Customer-facing sales deck:** a seller presents it to a prospect. The audience is the buyer.
- **Enablement kit:** teaches sellers how to run that conversation. The audience is the sales team, and the deliverable is the customer deck plus talk track, discovery questions, objection handling and proof. For the teaching part, also read `training.md`: sellers need practice, not a product lecture.

## What the evidence says, and how weak it is

Sales has little peer-reviewed research and a lot of vendor data. Use the following as informed defaults.

- **Conversations beat presentations [vendor data, correlational].** Gong's call analyses associate won deals with more balanced talk time, shorter stretches of uninterrupted seller monologue, and shorter intro-meeting decks (about 9 minutes of presenting in won deals versus about 11 in lost). Sellers who lean on slides in discovery talk more. Treat direction, not the exact figures, as the takeaway: build a deck that provokes dialogue.
- **Lead with the buyer's priority [vendor data].** Winning demos show the capability tied to the buyer's top problem first rather than following the product's structure.
- **Status quo is the main competitor [practice; some vendor research].** Many qualified deals end in no decision. The deck must make a case for change before a case for you. Note this applies to new customers; for renewals and expansions, reinforcing their existing choice works better than disruption.
- **Answer objections, do not dodge them [evidence].** Two-sided messages that raise and refute the counter-argument persuade better than one-sided ones (Allen 1991; O'Keefe 1999).
- **Proof from peers [practice, consistent with social-proof research].** A named, similar customer with a specific result is stronger than an aggregate claim.

## Customer-facing spine

1. **Their world, not yours:** the shift or pressure the buyer is under, in their terms. No company-history slide, no logo wall up front.
2. **The problem and its cost:** make the status quo expensive, with their numbers where discovery provided them. Leave room in the deck for a question here.
3. **What good looks like:** the outcome they want, described before the product.
4. **How we get you there:** the product, limited to the two or three capabilities that address their stated problem. Demo here, most valuable thing first.
5. **Proof:** a similar customer, the measurable result, a quote.
6. **Why us, why now:** the honest differentiators and the cost of waiting.
7. **The path:** what happens next, effort required from them, timeline, a specific ask.

Keep it short: plan about 10 minutes of presenting inside a longer conversation, with the rest in backup. Structure the deck modularly so the seller can jump to the section the buyer cares about. Company overview, security, pricing detail, integrations and more case studies go in an appendix the seller pulls from on demand.

## Enablement kit contents

For each customer-facing slide, the notes carry a **talk track**:

- **Say:** the point in conversational language, 30 to 60 seconds.
- **Ask:** a discovery or check-in question to hand the conversation back ("How are you handling that today?").
- **Listen for:** signals that tell the seller where to go next.
- **Proof:** the stat, customer or story to use, with its source.
- **Transition.**

A slide whose job is the buyer's answer ("How many days does one invoice take you?") works as a statement slide carrying the question; give its notes a cue such as `[45 seconds: the buyer answers; write the number down]`. Keep an internal, unsourced view of a competitor off the slides and in the seller guide, and label a next step you invented as your proposal.

Plus the rest of the kit. It does not go in the customer deck, which stays short and customer-facing: write it as a companion file in the deck folder (`SELLER-GUIDE.md`), or as a second deck when the enablement will itself be presented to the sales team. It holds:

- **Who this is for:** ideal customer, personas, their priorities, disqualifiers.
- **Discovery questions** grouped by persona.
- **Objection handling:** the five or so most common objections, each as: what they say, what they usually mean, how to respond (acknowledge, clarify with a question, answer with evidence, confirm), and the proof to use. Write responses as words a seller would say.
- **Competitive positioning:** honest, specific, including where the competitor is stronger and how to handle it.
- **Persona variants:** which slides change for the economic buyer, the technical evaluator and the end user (`audiences.md`).
- **Practice:** role-play scenarios and a certification check. Enablement that ends at "here is the deck" does not change behaviour.

## Honesty rules for this format

Sales decks attract invented proof. Use only customer names, logos, quotes, numbers and competitor claims the user supplied or that you can source. Otherwise write `[NEEDS: customer result with source]`. Never fabricate a testimonial, an ROI figure or a competitor weakness; sellers will repeat it to customers.

## Pitfalls

- Opening with "about us".
- A feature tour in product-menu order.
- Slides so dense the seller reads them aloud.
- One deck for every persona.
- Objection responses that argue instead of first acknowledging and asking.
- Proof with no source, which the first skeptical buyer will challenge.
- No explicit next step.
