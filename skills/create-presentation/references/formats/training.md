# Format: training, workshop, onboarding

The goal is that people can do something afterwards that they could not do before. Coverage is not the goal: material presented but not practiced is mostly not learned. This format has the strongest research base of all the formats in this skill, so follow it closely.

## What the evidence says

- **Active beats passive [evidence].** Across 225 studies of undergraduate STEM courses, students in lecture-only classes were 1.5 times more likely to fail than those in classes with active learning, and exam scores were about 6% higher with it (Freeman et al. 2014). Different population from workplace training, same mechanism.
- **Retrieval practice beats re-exposure [evidence].** Recalling material from memory produces better retention after days than re-reading it, even though re-reading feels more effective in the moment (Roediger and Karpicke 2006: about 61% versus 40% recall after a week). Questions interleaved into a lecture also reduce mind-wandering (Szpunar et al. 2013).
- **Spacing beats massing [evidence]** (Cepeda et al. 2006). Revisit earlier points later in the session and after it.
- **Worked examples before independent problems for novices [evidence]**, fading the guidance as competence grows; experienced learners need less scaffolding and can be slowed by it (expertise reversal).
- **Manage load [evidence]:** teach names and parts before how they interact (pre-training), break procedures into learner-paced segments, and remove nice-to-know material (coherence).
- **Learning styles are not a design input.** No good evidence supports matching instruction to visual, auditory or kinesthetic "styles" (Pashler et al. 2008).

## Objectives first

Write two to four objectives per hour, each as an observable performance: what the learner will do, under what conditions, to what standard (Mager). "Understand the escalation policy" cannot be observed. "Given a sample ticket, choose the right escalation tier and say why" can.

Each objective then needs, in the outline: a slide or segment that teaches it, a practice activity that exercises it, and a check that shows whether it worked. An objective without practice is a hope. Content that maps to no objective is a candidate to cut or move to a reference handout.

## Spine

A reliable sequence for each objective, following Merrill's first principles (problem-centered: activate, demonstrate, apply, integrate):

1. **Why it matters:** a real task or problem the learner will face. Show the finished result first.
2. **Activate:** ask what they already know or how they do it today. A prequestion they cannot yet answer primes attention to the answer.
3. **Demonstrate:** show it being done on a worked example, built up in steps, with the reasoning said aloud.
4. **Practice:** learners do it, first with support and then without. This is the core of the session, not a break from it.
5. **Feedback:** show the model answer, discuss common mistakes.
6. **Retrieve:** a short recall question later in the session, about material from earlier.

Session frame: opening (the task they will be able to do, the objectives, a prequestion) → two to four objective blocks as above → closing retrieval (learners restate or apply the key points with slides off) → what to do next on the job and where the reference material is.

## Time and slides

- Give practice at least a third of the session, and more for skills. If time is short, cut content, not practice.
- No stretch of pure telling longer than about 10 to 15 minutes without the learners doing something. This is a design habit backed by the active-learning findings, not a measured attention limit.
- Slides are sparser than you expect, because much of the time is activity. Activity slides stay on screen for minutes: they need the task, the time allowed, and what "done" looks like.
- Put reference detail (full tables, every option) in a handout or linked doc, not on slides. Slides teach; documents get looked up.

## Slide types to plan

- **Objectives slide:** phrased as "By the end you will be able to…", in the learner's language.
- **Worked example slides:** built with steps, labels next to the thing they describe.
- **Activity slides:** instruction, duration, expected output. Layouts with a large statement and few words work well.
- **Check slides:** a question first, the answer revealed on a step (`<Step at={1}>` around the answer; keep something visible in that region before the reveal so step 0 does not look broken). Make them think before they see it.
- **Practice cases are exercises, not evidence.** Sample tickets, scenarios and model answers you write to practise the user's rules are fine and necessary; label them as yours in the brief and the handoff, keep them clear of the policy's edge cases you had to guess at, and never present an invented example as the organisation's policy (a made-up worked calculation, a made-up form). When a rule cannot be taught without a detail you were not given, that slide is not ready: a visible `[NEEDS]` is the honest state.
- **Time.** The deck's `duration` is the whole session, and an activity slide's budget includes the activity. Put the timing in the notes as bracketed cues.
- **Common-mistakes slide:** the two or three errors beginners make, each with the fix.
- **Recap slide:** written as questions for the room, not statements to read.
- **Job aid pointer:** where to find the steps next week.

## Notes for this format

Notes are a facilitator guide: the exact question to ask, how long to wait, likely answers including wrong ones and how to respond, the timing for each activity, and what to cut if running late. Include the model answer for every exercise.

## Pitfalls

- Covering everything the expert knows instead of what the novice needs for the task.
- "Any questions?" as the only check for understanding. Silence is not comprehension.
- Recaps that re-show slides instead of asking people to recall.
- Quizzes that test trivia rather than the objective.
- Saving all practice for the end, when time has run out.
- Objectives that use "understand", "know", "be familiar with".
