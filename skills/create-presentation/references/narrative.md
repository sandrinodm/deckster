## 1. Start from the audience's question

A talk answers a question the audience has, or can be made to have in the first minute. Write that question down before choosing a structure. "Should we fund this?" "How do I do this task on Monday?" "Why should I care about this technology?" The structure is the shortest honest route from that question to your answer.

The speaker suffers from the curse of knowledge: once you know something it is hard to imagine not knowing it, and people badly overestimate how much of their meaning gets across **[some evidence]** (Newton's tapping study: tappers predicted listeners would name half the tunes, listeners got about one in forty). Design for what the audience knows at minute zero, not for what you know.

## 2. Choosing a structure

| The audience's question                   | Their starting state                       | Structure                                                         |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "What do you want me to decide?"          | Busy, senior, will interrupt               | Answer first (pyramid)                                            |
| "Why should I change anything?"           | Comfortable with the status quo, skeptical | Contrast (what is / what could be), or problem, solution, benefit |
| "What is going on and what should we do?" | Shares context, needs the logic            | Situation, complication, resolution                               |
| "How do I do this?"                       | Motivated novice                           | Instructional sequence (see `formats/training.md`)                |
| "What did you find and can I trust it?"   | Expert, evaluating                         | Question, method, findings, implications                          |
| "What happened?"                          | Curious, wants the lesson                  | Chronological story with a turning point, then lessons            |
| "Why you, why now?"                       | Evaluating a bet                           | Pitch sequence (see `formats/investor-pitch.md`)                  |

Structures combine. A common shape is a situation-complication opening that earns attention, a pyramid body that makes the case, and a contrast-style close that makes the future concrete.

One test applies to every structure: the read-through. The slide titles, read in order with nothing else, should make the argument.

## 3. The structures

### Answer first: the pyramid (Minto) [practice]

State the conclusion, then the three or so reasons that support it, then the evidence under each reason. Reasons at one level should not overlap and together should cover the case. A short situation-complication-question lead-in sets up the answer.

- Fits: executives, decision meetings, status reviews, any audience that may leave or interrupt after five minutes.
- Why it works: the listener holds the conclusion while judging each piece of evidence against it, instead of holding evidence while guessing where it leads. Stating the conclusion explicitly is also more persuasive than leaving it to be inferred **[evidence]** (O'Keefe's meta-analyses on explicit conclusions).
- Risk: with a hostile or unconvinced audience, an early answer can trigger rejection before the case is heard. For them, establish the shared problem first, then give the answer.

### Situation, complication, resolution (SCR / SCQA) [practice]

Situation: what the audience already accepts. Complication: what changed or what is at stake. (Question: what that raises.) Resolution: your answer and the plan.

- Fits: almost any business presentation; it is the default when unsure.
- Keep the situation short. It exists to establish agreement, and audiences resent being told at length what they already know.

### Contrast: what is, what could be (Duarte) [practice]

Alternate between the current reality and a better future, repeatedly, so the gap creates tension. End with a call to action and a concrete picture of the future once the action is taken. The audience is the hero; the presenter is the guide who equips them.

- Fits: keynotes, launches, change announcements, vision talks, sales openings.
- Evidence note: derived from analysis of famous speeches, which is selection on success. Treat it as a useful pattern, not a law. The underlying mechanism, that contrast and tension hold attention, is plausible and consistent with research on narrative transportation **[some evidence]**.

### Problem, solution, benefit [practice]

Make the problem real and costly for this audience, show the solution, show the benefit with proof. A variant adds "why now" before the solution.

- Fits: product presentations, sales pitches, proposals, short talks.
- The most common failure is rushing the problem. If the audience does not feel the problem, the solution is an answer to a question nobody asked.

### Question, method, findings, implications

- Fits: research readouts, technical evaluations, postmortems.
- Still put the headline finding near the start for mixed or senior audiences; the classic academic order saves the answer for last, which is the opposite of what a time-poor listener needs.

### Story with a turning point

A person with a goal meets an obstacle, tries, fails or struggles, something changes, and they come out different. Then name the lessons.

- Fits: conference talks, case studies, incident reviews, all-hands recognition.
- One real, specific case usually beats several summarized ones. Do not invent the case.

## 4. Openings

The first minute decides whether the audience grants attention. People remember the beginning and end of a sequence better than the middle **[evidence for lists; extrapolated to talks]**, so do not spend the opening on your biography, the agenda, or thanks.

Open with one of:

- **The complication:** the surprising fact, number, or change that creates the need for this talk.
- **A concrete case:** one customer, one incident, one user, told in three sentences.
- **The question:** asked the way the audience would ask it.
- **The answer** (for answer-first formats): the recommendation in one sentence.

Then give a roadmap in a sentence or a simple agenda, especially for talks longer than 15 minutes; advance organizers and signposting help comprehension **[evidence]** (Mayer's signaling and pre-training principles). Introduce yourself in one line only when the audience needs a reason to trust you, and put it after the hook.

Whatever the hook, it must be true and it must belong to the argument. A shocking statistic that the rest of the talk ignores is a trick, and audiences notice.

## 5. Closings

The ending is what people carry out of the room; evaluations of an experience lean heavily on its peak and its end **[evidence in other domains; extrapolated to talks]**.

- End on the message and the ask, not on "Questions?" or "Thank you". Take questions before the final slide, then close with the takeaway, so the last thing heard is yours and not the last questioner's.
- State the ask specifically: who does what by when. "Approve the pilot for two teams by 1 December" beats "let's discuss next steps".
- Recap in the same words used for the main points. Repetition of the core message is support for memory, not padding.
- The final slide stays on screen through Q&A. Make it the takeaway plus the next step or contact, because it will be looked at longer than any other slide.

## 6. Pacing and time

- **There is no evidence-based number of minutes per slide.** Rules like "one slide per minute" or "10 slides, 20 minutes, 30 point font" are opinions. Budget each slide by what has to be said over it, and let the count fall out. Sparse assertion-evidence decks have more slides, not fewer, than bullet decks for the same talk.
- **Check the pace, not only the total.** Count section slides in the budget. Dense slides (tables, comparisons, anything with more than one number) need 90 seconds or more for a senior or mixed audience. A plan where nearly every slide has under a minute reads as rushed even if the sum fits; remove slides instead of shaving seconds.
- **Plan for less time than you have.** Reserve Q&A explicitly, then leave about 10 percent slack. Talks run long, not short.
- **The "attention drops after 10 minutes" claim is weak.** A literature review found few primary studies behind it and concluded attention varies mostly with the teacher, not the clock (Bradbury 2016; Wilson and Korn 2007). What does have support is that attention lapses come and go throughout a talk and decrease after an active or changed segment (Bunce et al. 2010), and that interpolated questions reduce mind-wandering and improve learning in lectures (Szpunar et al. 2013). So do not insert gimmicks on a timer; do change mode at natural boundaries: from telling to showing, from slide to demo, from speaker to audience question.
- **Give the audience landmarks.** Section slides, a recurring progress cue, and verbal signposts ("that was the problem; now the fix") let people who drifted rejoin.
- **Mark what can be cut.** In the outline, flag the slides to skip if time runs short, so the presenter cuts an example and not the conclusion.
- **Speaking rate for sizing notes:** plan on roughly 130 to 150 words per minute **[practice]**, slower for technical content, non-native audiences, and anything with numbers.

## 7. Stories, examples and numbers

- Concrete examples and narratives improve understanding and can be persuasive through absorption in the story **[some evidence]** (Green and Brock on transportation). Whether stories or statistics persuade more is mixed across meta-analyses; statistics tend to move beliefs, narratives tend to move intentions. Use both: the number for credibility, the case for meaning.
- Acknowledging the obvious counter-argument and answering it is more persuasive than ignoring it **[evidence]** (two-sided messages with refutation; Allen 1991, O'Keefe 1999). Mentioning the objection without answering it is worse than not mentioning it.
- Put a human scale on big numbers: per customer, per day, compared with something familiar.

## 8. Folklore to ignore

Do not put these in a deck or use them to justify design choices:

- "Stories are 22 times more memorable than facts." No traceable study.
- "93 percent of communication is nonverbal" (7-38-55). Mehrabian's studies concerned conflicting cues about feelings using single words; he has said it does not apply generally.
- "People remember 10 percent of what they read, 20 percent of what they hear…" The percentages attached to Dale's cone were never measured.
- "Visuals are processed 60,000 times faster than text." No source.
- "Tailor to visual, auditory and kinesthetic learners." Reviews find no support for matching instruction to learning styles (Pashler et al. 2008).
- "Attention spans are 8 seconds" or "10 minutes". See above.
