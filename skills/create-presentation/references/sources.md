# Sources and evidence strength

This is the research behind the skill, grouped by topic, with an honest note on how far each source can be trusted. Compiled November 2026. Figures were checked against abstracts, publisher pages or the papers where accessible; where only secondary summaries were available it is noted. Re-verify exact figures before quoting them in a deck.

Evidence levels:

- **Strong:** many controlled experiments or meta-analyses, replicated.
- **Moderate:** a few controlled studies, or strong studies in a neighbouring setting.
- **Weak:** vendor data, correlational analyses, single studies, or extrapolation.
- **Practice:** practitioner frameworks without controlled tests. Often useful; not proven.

## Slide design and assertion-evidence

- Garner, J. and Alley, M. (2013). How the design of presentation slides affects audience comprehension: a case for the assertion-evidence approach. _International Journal of Engineering Education_ 29(6), 1564-1579. 110 engineering students; same narration, different slides; better comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived load, better delayed recall for assertion-evidence. https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-6/23_ijee2791ns.pdf **Moderate** (one research group, student samples, technical topics).
- Alley, M., Schreiber, M., Ramsdell, K. and Muffo, J. (2006). How the design of headlines in presentation slides affects audience retention. _Technical Communication_ 53(2). Sentence headlines improved recall over phrase headlines in a large lecture course. **Moderate.**
- Garner, Alley, Gaudelli and Zappe (2009). Common use of PowerPoint versus the assertion-evidence structure. _Technical Communication_ 56(4). **Moderate.**
- Aippersbach, Alley and Garner (2013), ASEE. Presenters who built assertion-evidence slides understood their content better. **Weak** (conference paper). A related ASEE follow-up reported a positive but non-significant trend.
- Research list: https://writing.engr.psu.edu/research.html ; approach: https://www.assertion-evidence.com/
- Kosslyn, S., Kievit, R., Russell, A. and Shephard, J. (2012). PowerPoint presentation flaws and failures: a psychological analysis. _Frontiers in Psychology_ 3:230. Eight principles; 140 slideshows audited; most common violations were bullets not built up (96%), more than two lines per bullet (91%), more than four bullets (91%); viewers are annoyed by violations but poor at identifying them. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00230/full **Moderate** (the audit is descriptive; the principles rest on established perception and memory research).
- Tufte, E. (2003, 2006). _The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint._ Argument and case study (Columbia accident briefing slides). **Practice** (influential critique, not an experiment).

## Cognitive load and multimedia learning

- Mayer, R. (2014). Principles for reducing extraneous processing in multimedia learning, and companion chapters, in _The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning_ (2nd ed.). Median effect sizes: coherence 0.86 (23 of 23 tests), signaling 0.41 (24 of 28), redundancy 0.86 (16 of 16), spatial contiguity 1.10 (22 of 22), segmenting about 0.77, pre-training about 0.75, modality about 0.76. https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/ **Strong** for direction. Caveats: mostly short lab lessons, novice learners, immediate tests; later editions and meta-analyses report somewhat different (often smaller) sizes; effects shrink or reverse with expertise, longer lessons and learner-paced material.
- Mayer, R. and Johnson, C. (2008). Revising the redundancy principle in multimedia learning. _Journal of Educational Psychology_ 100(2). Short on-screen keywords next to the graphic helped. **Moderate.**
- Sweller, J. Cognitive load theory (1988 onward); Sweller, Ayres and Kalyuga (2011), _Cognitive Load Theory._ **Strong** as a framework.
- Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_ 24. Working memory capacity of about four chunks. **Strong.**
- Kalyuga, S., Ayres, P., Chandler, P. and Sweller, J. (2003). The expertise reversal effect. _Educational Psychologist_ 38(1); Kalyuga (2007), _Educational Psychology Review_ 19. **Strong.**

## Attention, pacing and time

- Bradbury, N. (2016). Attention span during lectures: 8 seconds, 10 minutes, or more? _Advances in Physiology Education_ 40, 509-513. Few primary studies support the 10 to 15 minute claim; variation is driven mostly by the teacher. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/ **Moderate** (review).
- Wilson, K. and Korn, J. (2007). Attention during lectures: beyond ten minutes. _Teaching of Psychology_ 34(2). Same conclusion. **Moderate.**
- Bunce, D., Flens, E. and Neiles, K. (2010). How long can students pay attention in class? _Journal of Chemical Education_ 87(12). Attention lapses in short cycles throughout; fewer after active segments. **Moderate.**
- Szpunar, K., Khan, N. and Schacter, D. (2013). Interpolated memory tests reduce mind wandering and improve learning of online lectures. _PNAS_ 110(16). **Moderate.**
- Slides-per-minute rules, the 10/20/30 rule (Kawasaki), 18-minute talks: **Practice / opinion.** No controlled evidence found.
- Speaking rate of 130 to 150 words per minute for planning: **Practice.**

## Narrative structure and persuasion

- Minto, B. _The Pyramid Principle._ Answer first, grouped supporting arguments, SCQA introduction. **Practice.** No controlled tests found; only secondary descriptions were consulted.
- Duarte, N. (2010). _Resonate._ Contrast between what is and what could be; audience as hero. https://www.duarte.com/resources/books/resonate/ **Practice** (derived from analysing celebrated speeches, which is selection on success).
- O'Keefe, D. (1997). Standpoint explicitness and persuasive effect: a meta-analytic review. _Argumentation and Advocacy_ 34. Explicit conclusions and recommendations are more persuasive. **Strong.**
- Allen, M. (1991). Meta-analysis comparing the persuasiveness of one-sided and two-sided messages. _Western Journal of Speech Communication_ 55; O'Keefe, D. (1999), _Communication Yearbook_ 22. Two-sided messages with refutation beat one-sided; two-sided without refutation is worse. **Strong.**
- Green, M. and Brock, T. (2000). The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives. _Journal of Personality and Social Psychology_ 79(5). **Moderate.**
- Narrative versus statistical evidence: Allen and Preiss (1997), _Communication Research Reports_ 14; Zebregs et al. (2015), _Health Communication_ 30. Mixed; statistics affect beliefs more, narratives intentions more. **Moderate, mixed.**
- Newton, E. (1990). Tappers and listeners, Stanford dissertation, popularized in Heath and Heath (2007), _Made to Stick._ Curse of knowledge. **Weak as a single unpublished study**; the wider curse-of-knowledge literature (Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber 1989) is stronger.
- Serial position (Murdock 1962) and peak-end (Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier 1993). **Strong in their original settings; extrapolation to talks is inference.** Peak-end does not clearly hold for long experiences.

## Training and learning

- Freeman, S. et al. (2014). Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics. _PNAS_ 111(23). 225 studies; failure 1.5 times more likely under lecturing; exam scores about 6% higher with active learning. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060683 **Strong** (undergraduate STEM; transfer to workplace training is inference).
- Roediger, H. and Karpicke, J. (2006). Test-enhanced learning. _Psychological Science_ 17(3). **Strong** (replicated testing effect).
- Cepeda, N. et al. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: a review and quantitative synthesis. _Psychological Bulletin_ 132(3). **Strong.**
- Sweller, J. and Cooper, G. (1985). The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving. _Cognition and Instruction_ 2(1). **Strong** for novices.
- Merrill, M. D. (2002). First principles of instruction. _Educational Technology Research and Development_ 50(3). **Practice / synthesis.**
- Mager, R. (1962). _Preparing Instructional Objectives._ **Practice.**
- Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D. and Bjork, R. (2008). Learning styles: concepts and evidence. _Psychological Science in the Public Interest_ 9(3). No adequate evidence for matching instruction to styles. **Strong.**

## Data visualization

- Cleveland, W. and McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception. _Journal of the American Statistical Association_ 79(387). Accuracy ranking: position, length, angle/slope, area, volume, color. **Strong.**
- Heer, J. and Bostock, M. (2010). Crowdsourcing graphical perception. _CHI 2010._ Replication. https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/jheer-crowdflower.pdf **Strong.**
- Franconeri, S., Padilla, L., Shah, P., Zacks, J. and Hullman, J. (2021). The science of visual data communication: what works. _Psychological Science in the Public Interest_ 22(3). Review: integrate text with graphics, guide comparisons, zero baseline not always best, uncertainty for lay audiences. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/visual-data-communication.html **Strong** (review).
- Borkin, M. et al. (2016). Beyond memorability: visualization recognition and recall. _IEEE TVCG_ 22(1). Titles and text are attended and recalled most; message titles aid recall. **Moderate.**
- Kong, H.-K., Liu, Z. and Karahalios, K. (2018). Frames and slants in titles of visualizations on controversial topics. _CHI 2018_; and 2019 follow-up. Recalled message follows the title. **Moderate** (secondary summary consulted).
- Ajani, K. et al. (2021). Declutter and focus: empirically evaluating design guidelines for effective data communication. _IEEE TVCG_ 28(10). Focus drives what is remembered; decluttering mainly improves perceived professionalism; some trust cost. https://visualthinking.psych.northwestern.edu/projects/DeclutterFocus **Moderate** (six chart topics; authors caution on generality).
- Xiong, C., van Weelden, L. and Franconeri, S. (2019). The curse of knowledge in visual data communication. _IEEE TVCG_ 26(10). **Moderate.**
- Correll, M., Bertini, E. and Franconeri, S. (2020). Truncating the y-axis: threat or menace? _CHI 2020._ Truncation inflates perceived effect even when viewers know; no blanket rule; less of a concern for lines. https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.02035 **Moderate.**
- Bateman, S. et al. (2010). Useful junk? The effects of visual embellishment on comprehension and memorability of charts. _CHI 2010._ Embellished charts were no worse for interpretation and better recalled weeks later. **Moderate** (small sample; challenges strict minimalism).
- Skau, D. and Kosara, R. (2016). Arcs, angles, or areas: individual data encodings in pie and donut charts. _Computer Graphics Forum_ 35(3). Donuts about as accurate as pies; angle is the least important cue. **Moderate.** Spence and Lewandowsky (1991), _Applied Cognitive Psychology_ 5: pies are adequate for simple part-to-whole judgments. **Moderate.**
- Talbot, J., Setlur, V. and Anand, A. (2014). Four experiments on the perception of bar charts. _IEEE TVCG_ 20(12). Stacked and separated bars are harder to compare. **Moderate.**
- Isenberg, P. et al. (2011). A study on dual-scale data charts. _IEEE TVCG_ 17(12). Often cited against dual axes but tested a related design with 15 participants. **Weak** for the dual-axis rule; the argument against arbitrary scales is logical.
- Color vision deficiency prevalence of about 8% of men and 0.5% of women (northern European ancestry; lower elsewhere): standard figure, e.g. Birch (2012), _Journal of the Optical Society of America A_ 29(3). Okabe and Ito (2008) color-universal palette. **Strong.**
- Practitioner references that shaped the decision guide and checklist: Tufte, _The Visual Display of Quantitative Information_; Few, _Show Me the Numbers_; Knaflic, _Storytelling with Data_; Schwabish, _Better Data Visualizations_; Wilke, _Fundamentals of Data Visualization_ (https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/); Zelazny, _Say It with Charts._ **Practice**, largely consistent with the experimental work above. Tables versus graphs: Few; Gelman (2011), _Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics_ 20(1) (a deliberately one-sided essay).

## Sales, demos and investor pitches

- Gong Labs analyses of recorded sales calls (talk ratios, monologues, deck length of about 9.1 versus 11.4 minutes in won versus lost intro meetings, demo ordering; sample sizes vary by post, for example 67,149 demos). https://www.gong.io/resources/labs/ **Weak** (vendor, correlational, unpublished methods, figures differ between posts).
- Corporate Visions (status quo bias, "why change"); Dixon and Adamson (2011), _The Challenger Sale._ **Weak / practice** (vendor and proprietary survey research; reported no-decision rates conflict between sources).
- DocSend pitch-deck studies (time spent per deck and per section, by year). https://www.docsend.com/index/startup-fundraising/ **Weak** (vendor, selected sample, figures vary by year from under two to nearly four minutes).
- Sequoia Capital, Writing a business plan. https://sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan **Practice.**
- Demos in presentations: no controlled research found. **Practice.**
- Speaker notes, all-hands meetings, executive briefings as such: no direct controlled research found; guidance is derived from the general findings above. **Practice.**

## Claims checked and rejected

- "Stories are 22 times more memorable than facts": attributed to Jerome Bruner, no source found in his work.
- 7-38-55 rule: Mehrabian and Wiener (1967), Mehrabian and Ferris (1967) studied conflicting cues about feelings using single words; Mehrabian says it does not generalize.
- Percentages on Dale's cone of experience: not in Dale's work, never measured.
- "Visuals are processed 60,000 times faster than text": no source.
- "8-second attention span": refers to web-page behaviour at best; not supported.
- Learning styles: see Pashler et al. above.
